How fast did news travel?

Four great stories and {{source.papers|int}} American newspapers. Pick an event, press play and watch the news spread, day by day. Each dot is a paper; it fills in on the day its first report was printed. Tap one to read it.

    On the evening of 14 April 1865 Abraham Lincoln was shot at Ford's Theatre. By the next morning the news was in print in Cleveland, Nashville and New York, and the same day in Gold Hill, Nevada, {{ex.gold_hill.dist|int}} km away. But in Texas it took weeks. How fast did news really travel, and what slowed it down? I took four great American stories, from the telegraph age to the radio age, and checked {{source.issues|int}} digitised issues of {{source.papers|int}} newspapers for the first day each paper printed the news.

    The four are Lincoln's assassination (1865), Custer's defeat at the Little Bighorn (1876), the sinking of the Titanic (1912) and the attack on Pearl Harbor (1941). The pages come from Chronicling America, the Library of Congress's archive of digitised newspapers, as turned into searchable text by the American Stories project at Harvard. The map above plays each story day by day.

    01 · The answerThe telegraph had already won by 1865

    The surprise is how little changed. A daily paper that printed Lincoln's death waited a median of {{events.lincoln1865.daily_median|int}} day; so did a daily printing the Titanic in 1912 ({{events.titanic1912.daily_median|int}}) and Pearl Harbor in 1941 ({{events.pearl1941.daily_median|int}}). Once a story reached a telegraph office, it was in every connected city by the next edition, whether that city was in Ohio or Nevada. What took time was the last step: most American papers were weeklies, and a weekly printed the news when its press day came round. Weeklies took a median of {{events.lincoln1865.weekly_median|int}} days for Lincoln, {{events.titanic1912.weekly_median|int}} for the Titanic and {{events.pearl1941.weekly_median|int}} for Pearl Harbor.

    Median days from the event to the first report, for daily papers (filled) and weekly papers (open).

    So for most papers, the news missed no issue at all: {{events.lincoln1865.first_issue_pct|int}}% of the papers that reported Lincoln's death did so in their first issue after it, and {{events.titanic1912.first_issue_pct|int}}% for the Titanic.     02 · 1865A country split by war

    Lincoln died as the Civil War ended, and much of the South's telegraph and railway was wrecked. Papers in the states of the former Confederacy took a median of {{lincoln_conf.median|int}} days to print his death, against {{lincoln_conf.other_median|int}} days elsewhere. In South Carolina, where Sherman's army had passed weeks before, it was {{lincoln_conf.sc_median|f1}} days. The Texas papers in the sample took three to four weeks, and in Honolulu, then capital of an independent kingdom reached only by ship, The Pacific Commercial Advertiser printed it after {{ex.honolulu_lincoln.days|int}} days. Yet Gold Hill in Nevada, on the transcontinental line, had it the day after the shooting.

    Each dot is a newspaper's first report of Lincoln's death; red marks the states of the former Confederacy.

    03 · 1876Custer: the slowest mile

    The Little Bighorn shows where the real delay lay. The battle was fought on 25 June 1876 in Montana Territory, days from the nearest telegraph. No paper in the sample printed it for {{events.custer1876.first_any_days|int}} days. Then, on 6 July, it was everywhere at once: {{custer.first_day_n|int}} papers in {{custer.first_day_states|int}} states and territories printed it that day, from New York and Washington to Cheyenne and Nevada. Distance from the battlefield made no difference after that; the wire did not care. The Montana weeklies in the sample show it late, but their surviving pages are few, so that says more about the archive than about Montana.

    Newspapers printing Custer's defeat for the first time, by date and region.

    04 · DistanceFar did not mean late

    Plot each paper's delay against its distance from where the news entered the wires and there is almost no pattern among the dailies. In 1912 the two Honolulu dailies printed the Titanic news on 15 April, the day she sank, {{ex.honolulu_titanic.dist|int}} km from New York; in 1941 the Daily Alaska Empire in Juneau had Pearl Harbor the same day. The spread in each panel comes from how often the paper was printed, not where it was.

    05 · MethodHow I did this

    For each event the dataset holds every digitised page in a window of six to ten weeks afterwards, with a flag where the page text matched the event's name (Lincoln, Custer, Titanic, Pearl Harbor) and a short snippet round the match. A name is not a story: Lincoln County and Custer City were always in the news. So a page counts as reporting the event only if it is dated on or after the first day the news could have been printed and its snippet contains a word about the event itself (assassination, massacre, iceberg, attack, and so on). I read the first report of every paper that was early, short of a clear word, or might be about something else ({{review.reviewed|int}} snippets) and threw out {{review.rejected|int}}; in a random check of {{review.sample|int}} others, {{review.sample_ok|int}} were plainly about the event and {{review.sample_ambiguous|int}} was unclear.

    For each paper I then took the first issue after the news could have arrived and the first issue that reports it, counting days from the event and the issues in between. A paper counts if it has a digitised issue in the first fortnight ({{events.lincoln1865.eligible|int}} papers for Lincoln, {{events.custer1876.eligible|int}} for Custer, {{events.titanic1912.eligible|int}} for the Titanic, {{events.pearl1941.eligible|int}} for Pearl Harbor). A paper came out daily if its issues were at most two days apart, weekly if five or more. Each paper is placed at the town in its Library of Congress title, matched to Wikidata and GeoNames ({{source.geocoded|int}} of {{source.papers|int}} placed).

    Caveats
    • The digitised papers are a sample: what libraries chose to scan, with many more small-town weeklies than big-city dailies, and few papers for 1941.
    • OCR misses words, and the dataset keeps only text it could lay out as articles, so a paper may have printed the news an issue before the first page found here. Delays are upper bounds.
    • Only the snippet round the first name match on each page is checked. A page that reports the event further down, after an unrelated mention, may be missed.
    • Pearl Harbor was often reported as "Hawaii" or simply "the war", which the name match cannot see; that is one reason only {{events.pearl1941.reported_pct|int}}% of papers show it.
    • Dates are the issue dates printed on the paper. Weeklies were often printed a day or two before their date.
